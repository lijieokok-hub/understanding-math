Chapter 1: From failure configurations to a zero-entropy witness and ordered arrays

This chapter follows R Proposition 2.1 (pp.4–6) and §3.1 (pp.6–7). Starting from a minimal failure order on an arbitrary probability space, the source obtains a finite-alphabet zero-entropy factor and then ordered arrays. We develop the successive past-tail replacements, finite-entropy calculation, standard-factor coding, and compatibility of the probability laws at every level.

A joining here is a joint probability law with the specified coordinate marginals, invariant under the common time action. Independence of every proper coordinate subfamily of an arbitrary joining does not force all nonzero correlations into a zero-entropy factor. Each individual Bernoulli process has trivial past tail. A tail event is independent of every finite-coordinate cylinder; a monotone-class argument extends this to the full process σ\sigmaσ-algebra. This example is not a counterexample to R. It shows that the reduction must also use actual time separation within one system. R estimates the original time configurations directly before passing to joining or array limits.

Continuity of finite sums preserves nonnegativity, finite normalization, and consistency under deletion of coordinates. These are consistent finite-dimensional distributions on countably many finite-alphabet coordinates, so the probability extension theorem gives a unique law ρn\rho _nρn​.

Countable additivity can also be seen directly. The space XInX^{I^n}XIn is a countable product of finite alphabets, hence compact metrizable. Every set in its cylinder algebra is clopen and compact. A decreasing sequence from this algebra with empty intersection must already have an empty member by compactness. The finitely additive limit is therefore continuous from above at the empty set, hence a premeasure. Carathéodory extension gives a Borel probability. No general interchange of ultralimits with countable sums is assumed.

Bounded scalar ultralimits respect finite sums and products. Freeness also makes them agree with ordinary limits of convergent sequences. These are the ultrafilter properties used below; they do not interchange two limits.

8. Verifying each array property

8.1 Invariance under common time

Each finite-configuration law is invariant under simultaneous application of TXT_XTX​ to all cells: every time is an integer power of the same TXT_XTX​, and μX\mu _XμX​ is invariant under TXT_XTX​. The equality persists on cylinders in the limit. Since cylinders determine probability, ρn\rho _nρn​ is invariant under common time.

This is the diagonal action of one time transformation. Array indices are not extra time generators, and invariance under independent shifts of the cells has not been obtained.

8.2 Every fixed coordinate face has law ρn−1\rho _{n-1}ρn−1​

The finite-configuration law no longer depends on bℓb_\ellbℓ​ and retains precisely the same formula on the other n−1n-1n−1 axes.

Now take iterated ultralimits in their original order. The limit in the absent variable bℓb_\ellbℓ​ can simply be omitted. The remaining indices keep their relative order, giving exactly the definition of ρn−1\rho _{n-1}ρn−1​. No limits are interchanged.

Fixing any axis at any position thus gives the same lower-level marginal. There are countably many levels, and the identities come from one finite-configuration formula, rather than unrelated choices of limits at separate levels.

9. Ordered limits do not imply axis-permutation symmetry

The finite-configuration formula is formally symmetric under simultaneous relabeling of axes and bbb indices, but this does not interchange the iterated limits. The elementary scalar example 1{b1<b2}\mathbf1_{\{b_1<b_2\}}1{b1​<b2​}​ gives one when b2b_{2}b2​ tends to infinity first, and zero when b1b_{1}b1​ does. The distinction holds for any free ultrafilter, since the relevant sequences are eventually constant.

Compatibility of faces on arbitrary axes therefore does not make the full ρn\rho _nρn​ invariant under axis permutations. Mixing in Section 8.3 must act on the innermost variable bnb_nbn​, whose limit is taken first while the other parameters are fixed. That argument cannot be moved unchanged to another axis.

This is why §§3.2–3.5 still need saturated extensions and conditional locality. The initial arrays supply proper-subfamily product laws in the last-axis direction only.

10. The initial object and the classical tools used

Now take Y=XY=XY=X, use the identity as the local XXX factor, let the parameter UUU be a singleton, and set mn=ρnm_n=\rho _nmn​=ρn​. We have verified common-time invariance, arbitrary-face compatibility, and joint independence of proper families of last-axis planes. With constant parameters, the full-parameter conditional product law is the unconditional product law; independence of the local XXX from the parameter is automatic. This is an admissible starting object for Definition 3.1 and the later saturation construction.

The classical inputs used here have the following scope:

  1. Conditional expectation and reverse martingale convergence: on the same probability space, gi∈L1g_i\in L^{1}gi​∈L1 and F−m\mathcal F_{-m}F−m​ decreases to the defined completed tail C\mathcal CC. Convergence is in L1L^{1}L1; no standard original space is required.
  2. Increasing martingale convergence: conditional probabilities of finite atoms and finite two-sided α\alphaα-symbol windows with their σ\sigmaσ-algebras, used for past conditional entropy and approximation of the conditional entropy of β\betaβ.
  3. The finite-partition entropy chain rule and monotonicity of conditional entropy: all entropies are finite, and limits use bounded continuity on a finite simplex. The two entropy-rate inequalities in the zero-entropy reduction are written out in R and developed here.
  4. Simple-function or finite quantization approximation, product telescoping, and factor pullback: these give mixing for fixed bounded functions, fixed finite-valued approximations, and transfer of mixing, without an additional dynamical structure theorem.
  5. A free ultrafilter and the finite algebraic rules for bounded scalar limits; probability extension on countably many finite-alphabet coordinates, or the compact-cylinder premeasure argument above. These are used only to fix compatible array laws nonconstructively.

R's references mention Ryzhikov, Thouvenot, and de la Rue as historical precedents for the Pinsker reduction. The proof here does not use unexplained results from those references to fill a gap. In particular, it requires neither identification of C\mathcal CC as the maximal zero-entropy factor nor a finite-generator theorem. Historical attribution and the mathematical inputs actually used should be assessed separately.
