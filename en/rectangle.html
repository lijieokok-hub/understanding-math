A story in geometry · 01

Four corners
and a twist

Draw a loop without letting the line meet itself, except where it closes. Can you pick four points on it that are the corners of a rectangle?

A rectangle is guaranteed. Ask for a square, though, and the question is still open for an arbitrary Jordan curve. A small change in the question sends us a surprisingly long way.

01 / One continuous loop

The word “some” carries a lot of weight

Imagine reshaping a circle into a potato, a teardrop, or something so crumpled that a pencil sketch hardly does it justice. The only rules are continuity and no self-intersections. More precisely, a Jordan curve is the image of a continuous injection from the circle \(S^1\) into the plane. “Inscribed” asks only that the four vertices lie on the curve: its sides are allowed to pass outside the region enclosed by the curve.

Now make the challenge precise. In the first version, the curve may choose the rectangle’s proportions. In the second, you choose the proportions before seeing the curve. The difference between “some shape” and “this shape, please” is the hinge on which the story turns.

SOLVED

Some rectangle

Every plane Jordan curve contains the four vertices of a nondegenerate rectangle. Vaughan's proof appeared in Meyerson's article. [1]

OPEN IN GENERAL

A prescribed shape

Does every Jordan curve inscribe every specified rectangular shape, including a square? This remains open in general, although the smooth case has been solved. [2]

02 / Make a prediction

What do the diagonals know?

Here is the first useful change of viewpoint. Instead of checking four corners and four angles, look at the two diagonals. A rectangle’s diagonals share a midpoint and have equal length. Conversely, two distinct nonzero segments with those properties are the diagonals of a nondegenerate rectangle. The details are just below the experiment.

A circle gives us a friendly laboratory: any two distinct diameters already have the same midpoint and length. Before moving the slider, guess when the rectangle will become a square. Then test what happens as the diagonals nearly overlap.

Why do those two diagonal conditions suffice?

Write the endpoints as \(m\pm u\) and \(m\pm v\), where \(|u|=|v|>0\) and \(u\ne\pm v\). The four points \(m+u,m+v,m-u,m-v\) are distinct, and adjacent side vectors satisfy

\[(v-u)\cdot(-u-v)=|u|^2-|v|^2=0.\]

The common midpoint makes these four points a parallelogram; the calculation makes one angle a right angle, hence all four are right angles. In the experiment the two conditions came for free. On an arbitrary curve, finding two chords that satisfy both at once is the real work.

03 / A change of viewpoint

Give every chord an address

Following four points at once can feel like looking for four keys in a crowded room. Vaughan changes the inventory: regard an unordered pair of points \(\{a,b\}\) as one chord and give it an “address”. The first two coordinates record its midpoint; the third records its length.

\[F(\{a,b\})=\left(\frac{a+b}{2},\ |a-b|\right)\in\mathbb R^2\times[0,\infty).\]

If two different chords receive the same address, their common midpoint and length make them the diagonals of a rectangle. The question is now: must this address system have a collision? That is a precise reformulation, not a claim that the address of any individual chord is special. [1, p. 71]

Swapping names does not create a new chord. The motion also gives a hint of the global twist, but it does not by itself prove that the whole space of chords is a Möbius band.

The key assertion. For any plane Jordan curve, \(F\) must send two distinct unordered pairs to the same point. Here is the argument, with its topological inputs made explicit.

What space do all chords form?

Include zero-length chords \(\{a,a\}\). Since exchanging endpoints changes nothing, the parameter space is \((S^1\times S^1)/(s,t)\sim(t,s)\). A standard topological fact identifies it with a closed Möbius band; its boundary consists precisely of the repeated pairs \(\{a,a\}\).

Why a band at all? Start with two points on a circle and allow them to move. Close pairs live near a boundary: when they meet, the chord has length zero. Since the pair has no first endpoint, exchanging their roles does not create a new state. That identification gives the half twist. The drawing is a guide to the statement, not a proof of the homeomorphism.

Why does the seam reverse?

Write points on the circle as \(e^{it}\). For \(0\leq\theta,\delta\leq\pi\), let the point \((\theta,\delta)\) on our sheet stand for the unordered pair \(\{e^{i(\theta+\delta)},e^{i(\theta-\delta)}\}\).

Thus \((0,\delta)\sim(\pi,\pi-\delta)\): the top of the left edge meets the bottom of the right. Glue the two edges by this rule and the sheet becomes a Möbius band; its remaining boundary represents coincident endpoints. The dashed line indicates pairing; it is not a chord of the curve.

Every unordered pair is represented on the sheet, with no further identifications beyond the seam. The circle labels points on any Jordan curve; the curve itself need not be a circle.

Suppose there are no collisions

Suppose every chord gets a different address: \(F\) is injective. The band is compact and \(\mathbb R^3\) is Hausdorff, so a continuous injection is an embedding onto its image. The third coordinate now matters. Exactly the boundary pairs have length zero, so they trace the original curve at height zero; every other point of the band sits strictly above that plane.

Why do these two chords meet exactly?

Take \(\gamma(t)=(A\cos t,B\sin t)\) and use \(0\leq\theta,\delta\leq\pi\) for the unordered pair \(\{\gamma(\theta+\delta),\gamma(\theta-\delta)\}\). The displayed surface uses the actual map

\[F=\bigl(A\cos\theta\cos\delta,\ B\sin\theta\cos\delta,\ 2\sin\delta\sqrt{A^2\sin^2\theta+B^2\cos^2\theta}\bigr).\]

At \((\theta,\delta)=(\pi/4,\pi/2)\) and \((3\pi/4,\pi/2)\), two different chords have the common address \((0,0,\sqrt{2(A^2+B^2)})\). Their four endpoints are \((\pm A/\sqrt2,\pm B/\sqrt2)\): a nondegenerate rectangle. This is an exact example of a collision, not the proof for arbitrary curves.

A trap for the eye: a flat boundary alone does not force a self-intersection. The Sudanese Möbius band has a planar circular boundary and is embedded in three-dimensional space; its interior passes to both sides of the boundary plane. Vaughan's construction has an extra ingredient: every interior chord has positive length, so its image lies strictly above the plane. Only then can we attach the disk without meeting the interior. 3Blue1Brown's 2024 lesson draws attention to this counterexample too.

Cap the boundary

By the Jordan–Schoenflies theorem, the curve together with its enclosed region is a topological closed disk. Use that disk as a lid at height zero. The band and disk meet only at their shared boundary, because the interior of the band is above the plane. Gluing a disk to the boundary of a Möbius band gives the real projective plane \(\mathbb{RP}^2\).

A collision cannot be avoided

The supposed collision-free address system has therefore placed \(\mathbb{RP}^2\) inside \(\mathbb R^3\) without self-intersection. A standard theorem says this is impossible. So \(F\) must have a collision. There is still a detail to check: it cannot occur at height zero, because \(F(\{a,a\})=(a,0)\) determines \(a\). And two distinct chords with the same midpoint cannot share just one endpoint: the other would be forced to agree as well. We get four distinct points, hence a nondegenerate rectangle.

Four standard topological facts are used here rather than proved from the drawings: the identification of the pair space with a Möbius band; the Jordan–Schoenflies theorem; the identification of a capped Möbius band with \(\mathbb{RP}^2\); and the non-embeddability of \(\mathbb{RP}^2\) in three-space. See [1] and [3].

The argument does not locate a rectangle. It shows that a world in which every chord has a different address would force an impossible embedding.
04 / Beyond the proof

Why doesn't the same argument find a square?

The address recorded midpoint and length, but it did not record direction. So the collision produces a rectangle while leaving the angle between its diagonals uncontrolled. For a square those diagonals must also be perpendicular. The proof guarantees an answer; it has not promised the answer on our order form.

Could we smooth a rough curve, find a square on each smooth approximation, and take a limit? The plan has an escape hatch: those squares might shrink as the curves approach their limit, leaving four vertices at the same point. Without a lower bound that prevents collapse, compactness alone does not produce a nondegenerate square.

1911 · The square peg question

Toeplitz posed the question of an inscribed square for every Jordan curve; Matschke's survey provides historical context. [3]

1981 · One rectangle is guaranteed

Meyerson published Vaughan's topological argument for all plane Jordan curves, without prescribing the aspect ratio. [1]

2021 · Every shape for smooth curves

Greene and Lobb proved that every smooth Jordan curve inscribes a rectangle in each prescribed similarity class: the aspect ratio is fixed in advance, while size and orientation are free. [2]

2026 · The boundary moves

A preprint by Asano and Ike establishes every prescribed shape for a large class including all rectifiable Jordan curves. Another preprint by Greene and Lobb quantifies how many diagonal angles occur on every Jordan curve: if the enclosed area is \(A\) and the diameter is \(2R\), there is a set of attainable angles in \((0,\pi)\) with measure at least \(A/R^2\) (angles measured in radians). Both are preprints; even a positive-measure set need not contain \(90^\circ\). [4] [5]

As of 2 November 2026, the prescribed-shape problem for arbitrary Jordan curves, including the square case, remains open.

05 / A second balancing act

Lift four points. Then turn the dial.

Vaughan's rectangle argument appears in §4 of Meyerson's paper. Just before it, Theorem 3 asks a different question. Fix four distinct points on the unit circle and rotate them together. A continuous function \(f\) assigns a height to each point on the circle. Whatever the height function, at least two distinct rotation angles modulo a full turn make the four lifted points coplanar. Meyerson, Theorem 3, pp. 68–69

In the top view, the two diagonals cross. In space, the four lifted vertices are coplanar precisely when the diagonals reach that crossing at the same height. Over a complete turn, each base vertex visits the whole circle. Each diagonal height is a weighted average of its endpoint heights, with weights adding to one. Both diagonal heights therefore have the same circular mean, and their continuous difference has mean zero. Unless it vanishes everywhere, it must take both signs and cross zero at least twice on the circle. If it is identically zero, every angle works.

What is being counted? Rotation angles, not necessarily distinct unordered sets of vertices. For the square, a quarter turn brings the base back to the same set. And coplanar does not mean horizontal: this result is not a claim that an arbitrary Jordan curve contains two rectangles. It is a neighbouring theorem in the same paper, with a different conclusion.

Where does circle dynamics fit?

Milnor's Theorem 14.13 gives a related, independent tool. A continuous degree-one circle map \(T\) has no periodic points if and only if it admits a continuous, monotone, degree-one semiconjugacy \(h\) to an irrational rotation: \(h\circ T=R_\alpha\circ h\) for irrational \(\alpha\). The theorem even allows \(T\) itself to be non-monotone.

Think of \(h\) as a map that may compress information: an entire interval can be sent to a single point. A semiconjugacy is not automatically a conjugacy. It becomes useful when a circle transformation is part of the question; Vaughan's existence proof above neither uses it nor follows directly from it.
