One Loop, Four Corners | Inscribed Rectangles and Squares - Film Transcript

[01 - Must a Square Be Hiding?]
Draw any closed loop.
Now find four points on it that make a square.
On a circle, easy.
On a triangle, still possible.

[02 - Only the Four Vertices]
First, the rule.
Inscribed means only the four vertices sit on the curve.
Where the curve caves in, the edges of the square may pass outside it.

[03 - Easy Cases, Hard General Case]
Circles and triangles are easy.
But what about a loop you scribble freehand?
To this day, nobody knows.

[04 - 1911 · Toeplitz]
In 1911, Toeplitz posed the question.
It is also called the square peg problem, or Toeplitz's conjecture.

[05 - Switch to Rectangles First]
Attacking squares head-on is too hard.
So loosen the rules: ask only for a rectangle, and let the aspect ratio be whatever it wants.
That detour opened the door to topology.

[06 - Same Midpoint · Equal Length]
A rectangle's secret lies not in its four sides, but in its two diagonals.
They share a midpoint, and they have equal length.

[07 - Two Pairs Are Enough]
The converse holds too.
Take two distinct segments with the same midpoint, equal length, and four different endpoints: they are the diagonals of some rectangle.
Four corners become two chords.

[08 - A Pair → Three Numbers]
So give every chord an address card: the x and y of its midpoint, plus its length.
Three numbers in all.

[09 - Length Becomes Height]
Lay the first two numbers on the floor, and use the third as height.
Every chord becomes one point in space, and all the chords together form a surface.

[10 - The Boundary Is the Original Curve]
When the two ends meet, the chord has length zero, and its address falls back to the floor.
So the boundary of this surface is the original curve.

[11 - Intersection = Rectangle]
Two different cards at the same address mean two chords with a shared midpoint and equal length.
A rectangle appears.
What we are hunting is a collision.

[12 - A Collision Creates a Rectangle]
Put those two pairs back in the plane, and their four endpoints immediately form an inscribed rectangle.
Finding rectangles became finding self-intersections.

[13 - Why Must They Intersect?]
But why must it self-intersect?
The answer lies not in how the surface looks, but in how the pairs are arranged.

[14 - Ordered Pairs: A Torus]
First keep the pairs ordered.
They form a square sheet, and gluing opposite edges gives a torus.
But swapping the two points still describes the same pair.

[15 - Swap the Pair, Add a Twist]
So two edges of the sheet must be glued with a reversal.
Add a half twist, and the torus becomes a Möbius strip.

[16 - One Side · One Edge]
A Möbius strip has one side and one edge.
It is not decoration.
It is the home of all unordered pairs.

[17 - Cap the Boundary]
Now cap it with the disk enclosed by the curve, glued along the whole boundary at height zero.
The result is the real projective plane.
This step rests on Jordan's separation theorem.

[18 - Self-Intersection Is Forced]
The disk lies flat on the floor and cannot touch the strip above.
But the real projective plane cannot be embedded in three-dimensional space without self-intersection.
That is the contradiction.

[19 - Any Jordan Curve: A Rectangle]
So a collision is unavoidable.
Every continuous simple closed curve inscribes at least one rectangle.

[20 - But the Aspect Ratio Escapes]
Do not celebrate yet.
The proof guarantees a rectangle, but says nothing about choosing its aspect ratio.

[21 - Every Aspect Ratio?]
The stronger question is this: pick any ratio, flat or square or anything between.
Is it always there?

[22 - Lift to Four Dimensions]
Hugelmeyer lifted the pair surface into four dimensions.
Greene and Lobb then used symplectic geometry to force two surfaces to intersect.

[23 - Enter the Klein Bottle]
The glued object is a Klein bottle.
It cannot be embedded as a smooth Lagrangian surface in four-dimensional symplectic space, so the intersection points are forced.

[24 - 2021 · The Smooth Case]
That gave the 2021 theorem: every smooth Jordan curve inscribes rectangles of every aspect ratio, squares included.

[25 - The General Case Is Still Open]
But "the century-old conjecture is solved" is a misreading.
Only the smooth case is settled.
The general case stays open.

[26 - 2026 · Rectifiable Curves]
A 2026 preprint pushed the range to rectifiable curves: curves of finite length also inscribe rectangles of every ratio.

[27 - The General Conjecture Remains Open]
Beyond that lie general continuous curves.
They come with no guarantee of finite length, and no guarantee of a tangent.

[28 - Approximate, Then Take a Limit?]
Could we approximate one by smooth curves, and then take a limit of the squares?

[29 - It Can Collapse to a Point]
Careful.
The curves converge, but the squares may shrink all the way down, until the four corners collapse into a single point.
That is no longer a square.

[30 - Continuous ≠ Smooth]
Continuity only promises that the pen never leaves the paper.
Length may be infinite.
A tangent may exist nowhere.

[31 - Here Is the Boundary]
So the boundary is clear: every continuous curve has some rectangle, but a square for every curve is still only a conjecture.

[32 - A Simple Question, New Tools]
From four corners, to a Möbius strip, to four-dimensional symplectic space.
One simple question forced new mathematics into being.

[33 - A Century of Research]
Over a century of work, far more than three results.
On the square side, Emch and Schnirelman reached smooth curves.
On the rectangle side, Vaughan proved rectangles must exist in 1977, Greene and Lobb obtained every ratio in 2021, and Asano and Ike extended it to rectifiable curves in 2026.
For general continuous curves, the square is still a conjecture.

[34 - The Question Has No Full Stop]
Next time you scribble a closed loop, remember: you may be drawing a boundary that humanity has not yet crossed.
